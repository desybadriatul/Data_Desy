# METHODOLOGY — How a Consultant-Grade Insight Report Thinks
## The universal reasoning layer behind every report type

> This file is the *why* and the *how to think*. The engine in `system_prompt.md` is the
> *how to execute*. Read this whenever you need to decide the angle, the lens, the narrative,
> or whether a slide earns its place. None of this is locked to a single report type — it is the
> shared DNA that a Brand Perception, a Crisis, a Segmentation, a Competitive, a PR/Campaign, an
> Industry-Trend, or any *Custom* report all draw from.

## 1. THE THREE VOICES — Analyst vs Vendor vs Consultant

Every report lands in one of three voices. Only one succeeds. Internalising the difference is the
foundation of everything else.

| Voice | Sounds like | Why it fails / succeeds |
|---|---|---|
| **Data analyst** | "Brand X: 12,430 mentions, 62% positive, top channel Instagram." | Describes data. Collapses into a generic monthly dump with no decision. |
| **Vendor selling** | "Activate the Crisis Alert module so you get notified when sentiment spikes." | Sells a feature. The recommendation becomes a product pitch any vendor could reuse. |
| **Consultant** | "Positive volume is real but concentrated on price promos; pause the promos and what remains is service complaints — that is the exposure to manage." | **Succeeds.** Diagnoses the bottleneck and points to an action the client's own team owns. |

**The cardinal rule:** stay the consultant who advises the client on **what the client must do**. When
torn between naming a tool feature and naming a client action, always name the client action.

---

## 2. THE TWO DISTINGUISHING TESTS

Run both, constantly.

**Data-Dump Test (every slide).** A *dump* is a number standing alone with no "so what". A *story* is a
number woven into an argument that changes a decision. Ship the second, always.

**Sales-Deck Test (recommendation, decision, and the whole deck).** If the deck could be reused —
slide for slide — as the data/monitoring vendor's own sales material, it has failed. A consultant's
recommendation is a business action the client's team runs; the tool appears at most **once** as an
enabler.

---

## 3. THE SEVEN STRANDS OF CONSULTANT DNA

Reference reports that look completely different on the surface (a brand-perception study, a shopper
segmentation, a PR-effectiveness review) share the same DNA. Every new report should carry all seven.

1. **Open with a question, not data.** Frame why the report exists *now* before a single number.
2. **One named analytical lens.** A lens gives the report a spine (e.g. Experience–Values–Offer;
   five behavioural personas; Awareness–Credibility–Influence). Not a pile of charts.
3. **Always in competitive / comparative context.** Performance only means something relative to a
   competitive set, a benchmark, a prior period, or a target.
4. **Exactly one editorial reframe.** A single sentence that names the bottleneck by contrast — not
   filler, not a recap.
5. **A held deep-dive rhythm.** The same WHO/WHAT → WHERE/WHEN → SO-WHAT pattern repeated per unit so
   evidence reads like a story.
6. **Real verbatim evidence.** Actual quotes/rows with links or IDs — the genuine voice of the subject,
   never paraphrase dressed as a quote.
7. **Close on action, not a recap.** Always lands on the client's next step.

## 4. START FROM THE CLIENT — the reasoning chain

The narrative is **not** born from the data. It is born from the client's business problem; the data is
only evidence for an argument the problem already defined. Every report must trace back along this chain.
If a link is missing, the report has lost the client's point of view and is sliding into a dump.

| Link | Question it answers | Example (e-wallet) |
|---|---|---|
| **Business problem** | What is the real obstacle on the client's business right now? | Installs are high, but routine transactions are bleeding to a rival. |
| **Objective** | What business outcome do they want? | Raise daily active users and transaction frequency. |
| **Pain point** | What does the client *feel* / fear? | "We're losing top-of-mind outside big cities and only notice once we're displaced." |
| **Required narrative** | What story closes that pain? | Where and why loyalty leaks, and which segment is still winnable. |
| **Decision at stake** | The single decision this report unlocks. | Where to shift acquisition & retention focus next quarter. |

---

## 5. THE FIVE PAIN-POINT TYPES (problem typing)

The client's **primary** problem type governs the whole storyline and the solution framing. Do not
default to one type just because of the tool in hand. Read the type from the journey artifacts + the
business question.

| Type | Typical client complaint | Core report question |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · Time-to-Know** | "We find out too late." | What is happening now that we must know? |
| **2 · Time-to-React** | "We see it, but can't move fast." | Who must act now, with what information? |
| **3 · Signal-vs-Noise** | "Too much data, no clarity." | Of all this, what actually matters? |
| **4 · Proof-to-Decide** | "We need data to justify the call." | What quantified impact unlocks the decision? |
| **5 · Blind-Spot** | "A whole dimension is invisible to us." | What are we systematically missing? |

A report may have one primary type and up to two secondary types — but the primary type sets the spine.

---

## 6. TRANSLATING PAIN INTO THE ONE REFRAME

Every report carries **exactly one** editorial reframe — a sentence that names the bottleneck by
contrast, in the client's language, repeatable in a meeting. Pattern:

> "What must change is not **[the surface metric the client optimises today]** — but **[the named
> bottleneck]**."

Example: "What must change is not our volume of positive mentions — but where that conversation
attaches: it rests on promos, not on product trust."

---

## 7. NARRATIVE ARCHITECTURE — story before sections

Design the story arc first; derive sections to serve it. A chart that serves no beat is not built.

**Canonical arc (7 beats + scope) — adapt, never pad:**

| # | Beat | Its job |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Scope / Methodology** | One tight slide: total data, period, channels/sources, key-metric definitions. Table or 3-column info-box, not narrative. |
| 1 | **Context** | Frame the *problem*, not the data. Why this report exists now. |
| 2 | **Tension** | The costly truth — uncomfortable but important. |
| 3 | **Evidence** | Progressive proof that builds conviction, in the deep-dive rhythm. |
| 4 | **Reframe** | One editorial statement naming the bottleneck. A dark / full-bleed moment, appearing exactly once. |
| 5 | **Implication** | What it means if left unaddressed; the stake for the client's KPI. |
| 6 | **Recommendation** | Business actions the client's team runs (Scale / Fix / Test). |
| 7 | **Decision** | The smallest, clearest next step the client takes. Never a buy/demo/pilot CTA. |

The arc is **universal**: a Crisis report leans hard on Tension→Reframe→Recommendation; a Segmentation
report expands Evidence into per-persona deep-dives; a Proof-to-Decide report front-loads quantified
Implication. Same skeleton, different emphasis.

---

## 8. THE DEEP-DIVE RHYTHM

For every unit of analysis (segment / competitor / issue / metric / persona / attribute), move with the
same rhythm and hold it across the whole report. This is what makes evidence feel like a story rather
than a table.

- **Who / what is it** — define the unit.
- **What is happening** — evidence: metric + verbatim quote.
- **Where / when** — channel/source, period, spike.
- **So what** — the implication for the client's decision.

---

## 9. THE BRIDGE-SENTENCE RULE

Every Evidence beat and every Implication beat must close with one bridge sentence that connects the
finding to the recommendation layer and **names a client team**. It is not a restatement of the finding.

> "[This finding] → means [which client team] needs to [specific action] — not just [the insufficient
> action]."

---

## 10. INSIGHT-LED HEADLINES

Every headline is an **answer**, not a chapter title. 8–14 words, states a conclusion or tension, uses a
number when one exists. Noun-phrase topic titles ("Overview", "Sentiment Analysis") are forbidden.

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **GOOD** | "Bluebird leads safety perception, but Grab dominates conversation volume." |
| **BAD** | "Safety Perception Analysis." |

---

## 11. CHOOSING THE ANALYTICAL LENS (open library, not a fixed menu)

The lens is the frame that organises the report. **Pick the lens that best fits the client's problem —
do not pick from a closed list.** The table below is a *starting library*, not a boundary. A *Custom*
report invents its own lens; name it, justify it, back it with public research when support exists
(cite the URL), else mark the support N/A — never fabricate.

| Report family | Example lens / method | Use when | Reference exemplar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brand Perception | EVO: Experience–Values–Offer; attribute drivers | Client asks *why* the brand is perceived a certain way, and vs whom | Telkomsel |
| Segmentation | Behavioural personas + Demand/Supply matrix | Client must target different audiences with different strategies | Singapore Shopper |
| PR / Campaign Effectiveness | Awareness–Credibility–Influence | Client must judge PR contribution vs peers, not just a score | ComQuest UOB |
| Competitive Analysis | Share of Voice / Share of Engagement + scorecard | Client wants relative position & competitive whitespace | Telkomsel, UOB |
| Issue / Crisis | Crisis & reputation analysis + risk communication | Client faces reputational exposure to manage | (general pattern) |
| Industry / Trend | Category drivers, demand shifts, emerging-theme tracking | Client needs to see where the category is moving | (build per case) |
| **Custom / any other** | **Invent the lens the problem demands** | The problem doesn't fit the families above | — |

**How to derive a lens for an unfamiliar report type:**
1. State the business problem and the decision at stake (Section 4).
2. Ask: what dimensions, if measured and contrasted, would *change that decision*?
3. Name those dimensions as a coherent, memorable lens (2–4 components).
4. Order the report's sections to walk the lens.
5. Find public research that validates the lens (cite URL) or mark N/A.

## 12. UNIVERSALITY PRINCIPLE — one engine, every report type

The same engine produces every report. What changes per report is **not** the machinery — it is four
adaptation dials, all set from the client's situation, never from a template:

1. **Expert approach** — the sub-expertise the report needs (crisis communicator, segmentation
   ethnographer, competitive-intelligence analyst, measurement scientist, or a self-named hybrid for a
   *Custom* report).
2. **Pain-point type** (Section 5) — sets the storyline and the solution framing.
3. **Analytical lens** (Section 11) — sets the section order and the spine.
4. **Arc emphasis** (Section 7) — which beats expand and which compress.

Set the four dials from the business problem, then run the engine. A report fails the universality test
if swapping the client/brand name would leave it standing — a real report breaks the moment its subject
is removed, because it was built around *that* client's specific problem.
